No. Zetacoin is an open-source chain running since 2013 with a public ledger, a maintained client and checkable block production. Scams exist around it  -  fake downloads, fake support, fake airdrops  -  but those target people looking for the coin rather than being run by it.

## What makes a project checkable

A scam depends on unverifiable claims. Zetacoin's claims are verifiable in public: the chain height rises, blocks arrive every 30 seconds, the source is published, releases carry notes and checksums, and the supply is on a ledger anyone can read.

None of that requires trusting a statement. A synced wallet is itself evidence: it validates the chain and agrees with other nodes about its state.

| Claim | How to check it |
| --- | --- |
| The chain produces blocks | height climbs, blocks arrive every 30 seconds |
| The client is maintained | releases with notes on the repository |
| Supply is about 59.9 million | readable from the chain and public explorers |
| Coins are yours | keys in `wallet.dat`, no custodian |
| The swaps are closed | no tool distributed, unclaimed coins burned |

The full walk-through, with the checks in order, is in [Is the Zetacoin Wallet Safe to Use?](https://github.com/zetacoin-wallet/zetacoin-wallet-guides/blob/main/is-the-desktop-client-safe.md).

## The things that look like red flags, explained

Small market, thin liquidity, no major exchange listing, unsigned binaries. Each of those is a normal property of a small long-running coin rather than evidence of fraud, and each has a mundane explanation.

Thin liquidity reflects a small community. Absent listings reflect a coin that did not pursue them. Unsigned binaries reflect the cost of a commercial certificate for an open-source project. None of them involve anyone taking money from a new participant.

What would be a red flag is a promise: assured returns, a recovery service, a "migration" that requires sending coins, or a support channel that asks for a passphrase.

## The scams that actually exist

They cluster around the brand rather than the project. An imitation download page with a working installer. A direct message offering to recover lost coins. An airdrop that requires connecting a wallet or signing a message. A "support agent" who needs the wallet file.

Every one of those is an attack on a person, not on the protocol, and every one is preventable with the same habits: install from the release page, verify the checksum, ignore unsolicited messages, and never share a file or a phrase.

For the background behind this step, [Is Zetacoin Audited and Open Source?](https://github.com/zetacoin-wallet/zetacoin-wallet-guides/blob/main/is-it-audited-and-open-source.md) covers the same ground in more detail.

## What the project does not do

It does not solicit investments, promise returns, run a referral scheme, require a payment to unlock a balance, or contact holders directly about their wallets.

It also does not offer recovery of lost coins, and it cannot reverse a transaction or reset a passphrase. A message claiming otherwise is impersonating the project.

## A fair reading of the risk

Holding ZET carries market risk: thin liquidity, a small community and a price that can move on very little trading. Those are real and worth weighing.

Holding ZET does not carry custodial risk if you hold your own keys, which is a different risk profile from leaving a balance on a small exchange.

Confusing market risk with fraud is how people end up ignoring the actual danger, which is a fake download or a fake support message.

## Common questions

### Is Zetacoin a scam?

No. The chain is public and running, the client is maintained and the code is open source.

### Why is it so cheap and thinly traded?

Because the market is small. Price and liquidity reflect demand, not legitimacy.

### Did someone steal the old coins?

No. Unclaimed coins were burned to an address with no private key when the migration window closed; that destroys them rather than collecting them.

### How do I avoid the scams around it?

Install only from the release page, verify checksums, and never send a wallet file or passphrase to anyone.

## The difference between a bad market and a bad actor

A falling price and a thin order book are unpleasant, and neither is evidence of fraud. Fraud has a shape: someone asks for something and does not deliver.

In this ecosystem the shape is consistent. A page asks for a payment to unlock a balance. A message asks for a wallet file to "verify" it. A site asks you to connect a wallet to claim coins that do not exist. An advertisement offers to recover funds that were never lost. Each of those creates a victim by taking something, and none of them is the project.

The project's own material offers software, documentation and a chain to sync. It asks for nothing, which is the simplest test available: anything that asks is not it.

## What a holder should actually worry about

The realistic risks are a lost wallet file, a forgotten passphrase, a fake installer and a message from someone pretending to help. Those four cover almost every loss reported around small coins, and none of them requires the project to be dishonest.

Weighing market risk separately from security risk is what makes a decision coherent. A thin market may cost money on the way out; a bad download can cost everything at once. The second is preventable with habits, and the first is a property of the asset you are choosing.

For the preventive habits, {{ARTICLE:how-to-backup-zetacoin-wallet}} and {{ARTICLE:zetacoin-wallet-encryption-passphrase}} are the two that matter most.

## What a new holder should check first

The first check is not the price; it is the download. A wallet obtained from an imitation page hands the keys to whoever built it, and no amount of caution afterwards repairs that.

The second is the backup. A file with no copy is one disk failure from a total loss, and that outcome is far more common than fraud in this ecosystem.

The third is the passphrase record. An encrypted wallet whose passphrase exists only in memory is a wallet that ends with the person who remembered it.

Those three checks are unglamorous, and each has a page here because each is where the real losses happen. The dramatic scenarios get the attention; the boring ones take the coins.
